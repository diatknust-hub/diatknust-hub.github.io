Product Design

Designing the Future Before You Make It

Product Design is not one discipline among six at DIAT — it is the intellectual and technical spine that holds all six together. Every student, regardless of their chosen craft specialisation, studies Product Design from Year 1 through to final year. Before any student throws clay, cuts leather, welds metal, or carves wood, they have already designed what they intend to make — digitally, precisely, and professionally.

At DIAT, Product Design means learning to use Computer-Aided Design (CAD) software to produce technical drawings, 3D models, and rendered visualisations of craft objects before physical production begins. Students learn to write design briefs, specify materials, calculate dimensions, and present their concepts to a professional standard — the skills that allow a DIAT graduate to walk into any client meeting, gallery, or design competition and communicate their work with authority.

This CAD-to-craft approach — designing digitally, then making by hand — is what distinguishes DIAT graduates from craft practitioners who have not passed through formal design education. It is what allows the department's indigenous craft traditions to compete in the contemporary global design economy without losing their cultural identity or material integrity.

What You Will Learn

Skills & Techniques

Product Design skills at DIAT build progressively across all four years — from foundational drawing and digital literacy in Year 1 through to advanced design practice, professional presentation, and portfolio development in Year 4.

  • Technical drawing: orthographic projection, sectional views, and dimensioned drawings for craft objects
  • CAD software proficiency: producing 2D technical plans and 3D models of craft products prior to production
  • 3D rendering and visualisation: presenting photorealistic digital images of proposed craft works to clients and supervisors
  • Design brief writing: defining project scope, materials, dimensions, cultural references, and production constraints
  • Prototype development: producing scaled or full-size physical models to test form, proportion, and ergonomics before final production
  • Material specification: selecting, testing, and documenting appropriate materials for each craft discipline and intended use
  • Ergonomics and human-centred design: designing products that are comfortable, functional, and appropriate for their intended users
  • Professional presentation: assembling design portfolios, client boards, and exhibition proposals to industry standards

What Graduates Achieve
